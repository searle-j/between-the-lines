## TL;DR

- AI-driven job losses are a matter of choice, not inevitability. Employment falls because AI is used for automation, not because AI inherently reduces employment.
- We need a collective effort to help AI developers and users choose a direction aligned with socialist values: beyond automation, toward job creation and public well-being through full employment.
- This requires active government involvement, including designing human–AI collaboration, creating public-sector jobs with AI, and redistributing the gains from AI.

## Summary

### 1. Introduction

- At the 20th collective study session of the Political Bureau of the 20th CPC Central Committee, General Secretary Xi Jinping emphasized that AI brings unprecedented opportunities for development alongside unprecedented risks and challenges. In that spirit, we need to examine AI as both an opportunity and a challenge for national rejuvenation through Chinese modernization. *(Author's note. I've read quite a few papers, but an opening this intense is rare 🙂)*
- AI's impact on employment deserves particular attention. It has a dual effect: creating jobs while also eliminating them.
- But AI-driven job losses are not inevitable. It is more accurate to say that firms have chosen to use AI to cut employment—that is, to automate.

### 2. An Employment-First Philosophy

- In 1960, Norbert Wiener proposed the idea of "alignment," arguing that machines should act in accordance with human values. These values include the goals, preferences, and ethical principles of humanity as a whole or of specific groups.
- Which values AI should align with remains contested. The EU, the US, and other groups offer different answers.
- China has also issued codes such as the "Ethical Norms for New Generation Artificial Intelligence," calling for adherence to core socialist values: equal opportunity, rights protection, shared benefits, support for vulnerable groups, a human-centered approach, fairness, and justice.
- This paper adds an "employment-first philosophy." AI must contribute to people's incomes and well-being through full employment with good-quality jobs.

### 3. Rethinking the Motives Behind AI-Driven Technological Change

- What mindset would help AI developers and users pursue an employment-first direction? What would motivate them to create jobs rather than give in to the temptation to automate existing work?
- China has historically favored workers over machines because labor was cheap. But a shrinking working population has made labor more expensive, drawing firms toward automation.
- Reducing labor input, however, is not the only way to improve labor productivity.
  - AI will end the era in which productivity gains depend on reducing labor input. By making all resources—including labor—abundant, AI will leave little to gain in overall productivity from simply cutting labor input.
  - We should use AI to predict future demand and technology trends, then increase output rather than cut inputs.
- Nor should labor productivity be AI development's only goal. Creating work that meets social needs—in education, healthcare, and care services, for example—should also matter.

### 5. Reducing Incentives to Replace Jobs

- Despite all this, AI users still want to reduce inputs through automation. For them, cost-cutting can be a matter of survival. Lecturing them will not solve the problem.
- Governments should therefore make human labor as affordable as AI, helping workers remain competitive. This would give users a natural incentive to employ people alongside AI.
- To lower labor costs, governments should increase public spending. Covering medical bills, for example, reduces workers' living expenses. Total household income can then remain high even if firms pay lower wages.

### 6. Final Recommendations

1. Governments should create demand through new projects, with AI providing the supply. AI's high productivity enables governments to pursue more projects, ultimately creating more jobs.
2. Design human–machine complementarity well. Create jobs where people and machines reinforce one another. Alternatively, use AI to help less-skilled workers match skilled workers' productivity, allowing firms to hire more of them and raise total output.
3. Share the gains. China's economy grew as people moved en masse from low- to high-productivity jobs, such as from farms to factories. AI now threatens to reverse that movement. The public sector should absorb workers displaced from high-productivity jobs, rather than leaving them to move into low-productivity work. This makes it easier to share the wealth created in high-productivity jobs: redistribution to the public sector would face less resistance than redistribution to low-productivity work.
4. None of this happens automatically. Governments must keep every link in the chain intact: productivity gains → higher household income → more consumption. The gains from technological innovation need to be redistributed and then spent. For this to work, AI must be developed in ways that promote employment and increase output.

## Reflections

- The paper felt less like empirical research and more like "flattery + lectures + directives + come on, let's do better." (Cai Fang is both a scholar and a senior Party official.) I know too little economics to offer a proper rebuttal, but the emphasis on assertions seems to leave a few logical leaps. (Maybe?)
- As the paper briefly notes, China actively pursued robotic automation a few years ago, fearing that rising labor costs would weaken its international competitiveness. In 2023, China accounted for more than half of new robot installations worldwide. Now that AI-driven job losses look serious, policy seems to be taking a sharp turn.
- I was struck by the ambition to design an entire, enormous country around a single blueprint. Whatever one thinks of it, the idea of using public spending to lower labor costs and keep people as affordable as AI is astonishing. There seems to be a distinctly Chinese form of emergence at work.
- Will strong national demands for a specific technological direction improve technical capabilities? Probably not. But if alignment with social values counts as a technical capability, might China have an edge over the US there? Say, in building AI that follows the rules...
