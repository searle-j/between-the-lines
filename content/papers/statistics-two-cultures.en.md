- Statistical modeling has two cultures.
  1. Stochastic data modeling: assume a distribution generates the data and try to identify it.
  2. Algorithmic modeling: treat the data-generating mechanism as unknown and focus on predictive accuracy.
- Statisticians have focused almost exclusively on stochastic data modeling.
- They should actively embrace algorithmic modeling, which is growing rapidly outside statistics.

## Summary

### Introduction

- Given input variables $\mathbf{x}$ and response variables $\mathbf{y}$, we usually assume that some mechanism transforms $\mathbf{x}$ into $\mathbf{y}$. The two cultures differ in how they view that mechanism.

  | Culture | Data modeling | Algorithmic modeling |
  | :-- | :-- | :-- |
  | Goal | Model the mechanism itself | Treat the mechanism as unknown and focus on accuracy |
  | Model validation | Good fit with no unexplained patterns in the residuals | High predictive accuracy |
  | Example | If linear regression fits the data, infer that the mechanism has a linear structure | If a decision tree predicts well, understanding the mechanism is not essential |
  | Breiman's guess | The sole focus of 98% of statisticians | Only 2% of statisticians, but many researchers in other fields |

- The statistical community's excessive commitment to data modeling has had several consequences.
  - Questionable conclusions.
  - Neglect of algorithmic models even when they are more suitable.
  - Missed opportunities to tackle interesting new problems.

### The Drawbacks of Data Models

- Data models have delivered many important results. But insisting on them has also caused problems.
  - **Too much emphasis on statistical significance:** many studies jump to conclusions simply because an analysis produces a significant result. Even statistics textbooks warn against this. Researchers should focus on the problem, not just the model's statistical significance, yet they often care more about the latter.
  - **Statistical significance itself:** simulation studies at the time showed that goodness-of-fit measures *(Author's note. Numerical measures of how well a model accounts for the data; many approaches have been proposed.)* and residual analysis do not always behave as expected. For example, a measure intended to detect nonlinearity may fail to flag data generated by a nonlinear process.
  - **Predictive accuracy:** measures such as cross-validation are not widely used, despite being standard practice in machine learning.
  - **Simple assumptions:** a commitment to data models ties statistical tools to overly simple assumptions, such as multivariate normality. These assumptions are unlikely to hold for complex data. Trying to explain such data with simple, interpretable models can lead to dubious assumptions and conclusions. An algorithmic approach may be more suitable. The point is not that algorithms are always better, but that we should choose tools to suit the problem.
  - **The future of statistics:** neglecting algorithmic approaches risks leaving statisticians behind as computing advances—and shutting them out of some of the most interesting statistical problems.

### Algorithmic Modeling

- Algorithmic modeling offers an alternative to data modeling.
- In fact, it has long been studied within statistics under various names.
- More recently, it has developed in a new research community, spurred by powerful algorithms such as neural networks and decision trees that emerged in the mid-1980s.
- This community focuses on problems requiring accurate predictions, such as finance and weather forecasting, and rarely uses data models.
- It also places less emphasis on interpretability: if a method predicts well, an unexplained result is acceptable.
- Its advances have been remarkable. Three shifts in perspective stand out.
  1. **Rashomon:** there is more than one good model. Many models can fit the same data about equally well, yet data modeling tries to identify and report a single best model. Assuming that such a model exists is itself unrealistic. Algorithmic modeling is more practical: it only needs to improve predictive accuracy, not identify the optimal model.
  2. **Occam:** simplicity does not guarantee accuracy. Decision trees, for example, are relatively simple and interpretable. But when necessary, we should be willing to trade interpretability for the greater predictive accuracy of a random forest.
  3. **Bellman:** the curse of dimensionality can be a blessing. Data-modeling methods struggle with high-dimensional data, so they tend to retain only essential features. Algorithmic methods instead see more features as more information that can improve performance. Handwriting and speech recognition illustrate this blessing of dimensionality.

### Information from a Black Box

- We began by associating data modeling with interpretability and algorithmic modeling with predictive accuracy.
- But the two are not necessarily a trade-off. If an algorithmic model predicts substantially better than a data model, it should be seen as providing more information about the phenomenon.
- Suppose a data model uses a few important variables selected from thousands, while an algorithmic model uses all of them and predicts better. Using the information across all those variables should count as a better "explanation."

### Conclusion

- We are in an era of terabytes of data. Insisting on data models alone makes little sense. It is time to broaden statistics by staying open to a wider range of methods.

***What follows covers the published comments on the paper and Breiman's rejoinder.***

### D. R. Cox's Comments

1. Statistics has never been confined to probabilistic methods. Algorithmic modeling has always been part of the field.
2. Widespread misuse of statistical methods does not make the methods themselves wrong.
3. As Breiman argues, some tasks care mainly about predictive accuracy, such as economic and flood forecasting. But we often need predictions under conditions different from those in the data—for example, the incidence of a disease variant. For these more ambitious tasks, understanding the underlying mechanism through plausible scientific hypotheses matters just as much as exploiting patterns to improve accuracy.
4. Understanding a mechanism also helps us ask the next scientific question. Even an incorrect conclusion can advance science if the attempt to formalize the mechanism leads somewhere useful.
5. An old saying captures the point: "Better a rough answer to the right question than an exact answer to the wrong one."

### Brad Efron's Comments

1. Since Fisher, statistics has spent a century centered on unbiasedness. *(Author's note. An estimator is unbiased when it estimates the parameter correctly on average: $E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta$.)* Machine-learning methods now tackle problems that lack the favorable conditions assumed for unbiased estimation, such as enough observations relative to the number of parameters. These methods can be useful for particular problems, but we should recognize the bias lurking in all their estimates as they give up unbiasedness to reduce error.
2. Complex methods are harder to criticize than simple ones. Once we call something a black box, how are we supposed to tell what is wrong inside it? Statisticians should help unpack these mechanisms and make them understandable, advancing the field. Machine learning may be enough to recognize messy handwriting, but disease research calls for a deeper understanding of the underlying phenomenon.

### Leo Breiman's Rejoinder

#### To D. R. Cox

1. My approach is not simply, "Here is a lot of data; let's do something with it." Rather, I would put predicting future events ahead of scientific hypotheses.
2. As Cox notes, simple data models can be more useful in some settings, especially when data are scarce. But that does not justify using them exclusively. Algorithmic models have proven useful far beyond Cox's examples of economic and flood forecasting, including covariate analysis with random forests and highly complex pattern recognition.
3. In one example in his book, Cox validates the model using only $R^2$ and residual analysis. This is exactly the questionable use of data models I am criticizing: once a model fits reasonably well, little further justification is offered. We need additional measures, such as predictive accuracy. I tried to obtain the data for an alternative analysis but could not. Comparing our approaches would have been interesting.
4. Statisticians should be more pragmatic instead of insisting on data models alone. As the paper argues, growing data sets are breaking the assumptions of traditional methods. Statistics needs to expand by embracing new approaches, especially computational ones.

#### To Brad Efron

1. Efron worries about overly complex models. But science moves from simple to complex: relativity, for example, is more complex than Newtonian mechanics. Accepting complexity to describe reality accurately seems more scientific to me.
2. My attitude is also practical. Before attending NeurIPS in 1992, I was skeptical of neural networks and AI. I left a believer because neural networks solved problems other models could not. I support them not because their complexity makes them hard to criticize, but because they get the job done.

## Reflections

- I read this after suddenly wondering what statistics actually is. Now I am less sure than before. In the broadest sense, is it the collection of methods that only come into play when we have multiple cases rather than one—and therefore patterns and uncertainty?
- About forty years before this paper, John Tukey offered his own thoughts on the future of statistics in "The Future of Data Analysis." Roughly speaking:
  - Data analysis is an empirical science, not mathematics. Its justification comes from usefulness, not proof.
  - Do not obsess over optimality. A reasonably good answer has value if it is well defined and solves a real problem.
  - Good data analysis involves judgment. Mechanically reading patterns is not enough; the analyst's judgment matters.
  - Data should lead to new questions. Exploring data, discovering and testing hypotheses, and finding leads for further exploration should form a connected process.
  - Study messy, real-world data, not just data that satisfy tidy assumptions.
  - Computers and simulations will change how statistics is done. Computational data processing will reveal new insights.
