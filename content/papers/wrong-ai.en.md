## TL;DR

- Innovative technologies reduce demand for existing labor but also open up new industries and create new labor demand, ultimately raising total output.
- AI has the potential to do the same.
- But none of this happens just because we sit back and watch. We need to steer AI toward job creation and greater output, not automation.

## Summary

### Introduction

- AI is a promising technology.
- But are we developing it in ways that maximize output? The answer is far from clear, which makes now a good time to ask.

### AI as a Technology Platform

- AI is a "technology platform."
- A technology platform is not a tool with a single function, but a foundation for many applications. If a technology can serve many purposes, its social impact depends less on the technology itself than on what we develop it for.
- The point is this: "AI-driven declines in employment and wages are not inevitable; they are the result of choices." If we develop AI to automate rather than to raise output through innovation, it will shrink employment rather than create it.

### Labor and Technology

- We often expect new technologies to create more jobs than they eliminate, ultimately increasing total output.
- The reality is this: new technologies do replace existing work while creating new labor demand. But less innovative technologies eliminate jobs by automating existing work. More innovative technologies go beyond automation, opening up new industries and creating jobs.
- The key word is "automation." Its goal is to reduce inputs, not raise output. The same output then requires fewer workers, reducing employment and wages. Society gains only limited benefits while inequality deepens. (Vulnerable groups are hit harder by this inequality.)

### A History of Automation

- So is automation always bad?
- Automation has been present at every major turning point in the history of productivity, including the Industrial Revolution.
- If automation were that bad, the value of labor should have fallen every time it was introduced. By now, that value should be near zero, with almost no jobs left. Yet the labor share *(Author's note. The share of national income paid to labor. A decline in this share means that the value of labor has fallen.)* has remained roughly stable over the past 200 years. This suggests that technology has gone beyond automation to generate new labor demand. Put simply, new jobs with higher productivity than the jobs lost to automation have kept people in demand.
- The problem, then, is not automation itself. It is uninnovative technology that stops at automation—or treats automation as the goal—instead of going on to create new work.

### What AI Could Do

- We can imagine AI developed with innovation, not automation, as its ultimate goal. For example:
  - Education: AI could enable highly personalized education. Teachers give up on personalization because they doubt they can do it well, even with considerable time and effort. But if AI assistance makes genuine personalization look feasible, they might bring in more educators to make it happen.
  - Healthcare: digital technologies and AI are entering healthcare, but often not in ways that increase output and employment—pathology image analysis, for example. If AI could gather and analyze vast amounts of information, more healthcare workers might be needed to act on it.
  - Augmented reality: robots tend to replace people rather than increase employment for two main reasons. First, working near robots is dangerous. Second, people cannot work with the same precision as robots. Augmented reality, however, could let people work near robots and move their arms with comparable precision. Enabling this kind of human–robot collaboration could increase both employment and total output.

### Why Do We Build the Wrong Kind of AI?

- If innovative technologies increase output, shouldn't output-hungry firms choose to develop AI that way? Unfortunately, firms pass up innovation for several reasons.
  - Innovation benefits competitors too. Innovators cannot capture all the gains themselves.
  - Firms play it safe when they perceive the risks of innovation as outweighing its expected rewards.
  - Firms are often shortsighted, preferring short-term input reductions to long-term output growth. Governments should counter this tendency and encourage long-term social benefits. Yet the US government now lacks both the funding and the research capacity to exert much influence over Silicon Valley.
  - Governments tax employment but not automation, such as robots. Tax savings can be another incentive to automate.
  - Culture matters too. As firms focus on automation, researchers and engineers come to see being good at automation as cool. Aspiring researchers and engineers naturally follow suit. Automation becomes the spirit of the times, reinforcing firms' focus on it.
- There is not yet enough evidence that firms invest more in the wrong kind of AI than in the right kind. But the incentives to build the right kind are clearly lacking.

### The Social Impact of the Wrong Kind of AI

- Left unchecked, the wrong kind of AI will slow output growth, shrink employment, lower wages, and increase inequality.
- This could have undesirable social consequences. Populist parties might gain ground, or individual business leaders might acquire excessive political influence. Firms benefiting from automation might also obstruct innovation by new entrants.

### Conclusion

- We should not assume that AI will naturally develop in a beneficial direction. There are far too many ways it could go wrong. This essay examines one of them through an economic lens.
- Before it is too late, we need to ask what the right direction for AI is, from multiple perspectives.

## Reflections

- I was surprised to see these ideas laid out back in 2018–2019. Is this the kind of insight it takes to win a Nobel Prize? 🤔
- Do we choose the direction of technological development? Could humanity have chosen to develop nuclear energy without building nuclear weapons? Not sure.
- Is creating more jobs really the right direction for AI? Wouldn't it be better to raise output enough that everyone could work half as much?
- As of 2026, AI seems to have changed the job market in three main ways.

  1. Jobs really are disappearing: call center agents, for example.
  2. Jobs really are emerging: AI builders and forward-deployed engineers, for example.
  3. Each person is expected to do more work: "You can do all that with AI, right?"

  - The first two are apparently features of every new technology, so fair enough. But how do we explain the third? Is AI developing in a direction that makes a small workforce do everything, rather than creating more jobs? However innovative the new work might be, could AI not do that too?
- As someone with little background in economics, I came away with more questions than answers. (What is this, and what is that?) What did resonate was the conclusion: we need to keep asking what the right kind of AI looks like.

---

## Takeaways

- A successful AI project may need to go beyond replacing existing work and actually increase output. Automation is a stop along the way, not the destination.
- Keep AI's social impact in mind!
