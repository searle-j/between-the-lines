## Bibliographic Information

- title: The True Creator of Everything: How the Human Brain Shaped the Universe as We Know It
- authors: Miguel Nicolelis
- publication year: 2020

## TL;DR

- The world we experience is not the external world itself, but the result of the brain organizing and interpreting sensory information.
- Nicolelis argues that the brain's information processing is analog (i.e., continuous) and inseparable from the physical body, making it impossible to fully simulate the brain with a Turing machine.
- Furthermore, he argues that "connections," from those between individual neurons to those between individual brains, give rise to cognition and consciousness, and that even concepts such as time, space, causality, and mathematics are inventions of the brain for understanding the world.

## Summary

### The Brain and the World

- Gombrich said, "There really is no such thing as Art. There are only artists." Put differently, it means this: "There is no fixed and universal essence called art." We retrospectively group together the countless activities of artists and give them the name "art."
- Similarly, Miguel Nicolelis argues in this book, "There is no world, there are only brains." Our brains retrospectively bundle together the vast amounts of information coming in from the environment and perceive the result as "the world."

### The Evolution of the Brain

- The neocortex of the human brain is remarkably large.
  - The human neocortex developed under evolutionary pressure to handle complex social interactions. There is also a hypothesis that language developed to help us efficiently interact with many people at the same time.
  - The "gene-culture coevolution" hypothesis likewise suggests that social interaction promoted the expansion of the neocortex.
  - Unlike those of our other primate relatives, the human thumb moved toward the outside of the hand, which is also a factor that helps explain the size of the neocortex.
- However, the human neocortex contains not only a great deal of gray matter *(Author's note. Tissue consisting mainly of neuronal cell bodies; responsible for information processing. Gray in color.)*, but also a great deal of white matter *(Author's note. Tissue consisting mainly of neuronal axons; responsible for information transmission. White in color.)*.
  - Nicolelis believes that consciousness originates from connections between information—in other words, from white matter. This is his personal hypothesis for the binding problem (i.e., how information processed separately across brain regions is integrated into a unified perceptual experience).

### The Brain and Information

- Nicolelis proposes the concepts of Shannon Information and Gödel Information in the following sense.
  - Shannon Information: Digital information that can be represented precisely in 0s and 1s. All modern computers (i.e., Turing machines) operate based on this digital information. e.g., an image made of pixels
  - Gödel Information *(Author's note. To me, Gödel Information seems less like a rigorously defined concept and more like... everything that Shannon Information fails to capture.)*: Analog information that cannot be represented precisely in 0s and 1s. Because it has infinite precision (i.e., because it is continuous), modern computers cannot process it perfectly. e.g., the physical state of a mercury thermometer
- Three reasons why modern computers cannot handle analog information:
  1. Countable vs. uncountable sets: A Turing machine can handle only countable sets such as rational numbers. It cannot process uncountable sets such as irrational numbers. Therefore, it cannot represent an instantaneous analog state.
  2. Chaos: Of course, uncountable sets can be processed approximately. However, an initial approximation error grows exponentially as computations accumulate.
  3. Temporal continuity: Time itself is continuous and therefore cannot be represented by a Turing machine in the first place, making it impossible to represent analog changes over the continuous flow of time.
- The example of awe: Why can we not copy the feeling of awe and display it on a computer? Because our brains are analog, and the information called awe is also analog, so it cannot be represented by modern computers.
- Of course, the brain also uses digital information. Nicolelis's argument, however, is that such Shannon Information accounts for only part of brain activity. Therefore, truly simulating the brain with a modern computer is impossible.

### Other Limitations of Turing Machines

- Hardware and software: Living organisms have no distinction between hardware and software. In other words, the mind is part of the physical body, and the physical state of the body itself gives rise to the information-processing process we call the mind. By contrast, the physical state of a Turing machine is entirely unrelated to information processing, so a Turing machine without software is nothing more than scrap metal incapable of processing information. A more intuitive example can be found in a question posed by John Searle: *"Why doesn't software that simulates photosynthesis almost perfectly actually produce carbohydrates?"* Going further, if we someday succeed in perfectly simulating the human brain with a Turing machine, we might ask: *"Why doesn't software that simulates human information processing almost perfectly actually produce a mind?"*
- Multilevel chaos: Above, I already mentioned "Turing-machine-style computation in which initial approximation errors explode." But the brain's chaos does not end there. The brain is chaotic at multiple levels: 1) the brain itself is chaotic, 2) the evolutionary process of the brain is chaotic, and 3) the interaction between the brain and the environment is chaotic. Therefore, if a Turing machine cannot even handle the chaos of the brain itself, it would be unable to model this kind of multilevel chaos.
- As an aside, Turing himself said something along these lines: *"I often develop my logic by assuming the existence of an 'oracle' that already knows everything. But if such an oracle actually existed, what would it be? At least, I don't think it would be a machine..."*

### The Relativistic Brain Theory

- Shifting the topic slightly, let us answer not "Why do Turing machines fail?" but "How does the brain succeed?"
- Neurons transmit information through electrochemical signals. These electrical signals also generate tiny electromagnetic fields around the neurons. Nicolelis believes that these electromagnetic fields remotely transmit information to other neurons that are not connected by axons. He argues that this remote transmission acts as a "physiological glue," aligning neurons and synchronizing neural activity, ultimately giving rise to consciousness and cognition.
  - It is already widely known from examples such as TMS that electromagnetic fields can intervene in neural activity.

### Brain Net

- Nicolelis proposes Brain Net as a somewhat radical concept. Brain Net is an extension of the Relativistic Brain Theory. Just as individual neurons remotely exchange information through electromagnetic fields, individual brains may also exchange information through "a mechanism that remains unknown."
- Experiments demonstrating the possibility of Brain Net:
  - Brain-to-brain communication: One rat (encoder) can send a binary signal through a brain interface to another rat (decoder), telling it which of two levers to press to receive a reward.
  - Brain Net experiment: Three macaque monkeys participated in the experiment in separate rooms. Let us call them A, B, and C. To receive a reward (a drop of grape juice), the monkeys had to move a virtual avatar arm on a screen to a specific point using implanted brain chips. The virtual avatar arm moved in 3D space: A controlled the x- and y-axes, B controlled the y- and z-axes, and C controlled the z- and x-axes. Thus, each axis was controlled by the thoughts of two monkeys. After three weeks of practice, they successfully performed this mental three-legged race.
    - If one monkey slacked off and did nothing, the other two could adjust their signals and eventually succeed, much like other parts of the brain can compensate when a particular part is damaged.
    - If the free-riding monkey was denied its reward, it immediately began working hard again on the next turn. In other words, the monkeys clearly knew what kind of control they were exerting over the virtual avatar arm.
- Brain Net may already exist.
  - Mirror neuron experiment: a rider monkey and an observer monkey. The rider monkey travels around a space in a wheelchair to find a reward. The observer monkey simply watches the rider. Yet merely by watching, the neural activities in their motor regions become synchronized.
  - Therefore, we may be creating a Brain Net simply by looking at one another.

### Other Interesting Ideas

- Time and space, and even mathematical concepts, are all products of the brain.
  - Leibniz is said to have argued: The universe is merely a set of connections among things. Space does not exist. It is merely the connections among objects. Time does not exist either. It is merely the sequential connection of events.
  - Some quantum physicists also say: If time and space differ depending on the observer, and even causality could be reversed (some delayed-choice experiments leave room for the interpretation that future events could determine past events), then our humble cognition—which assumes that there is one and only one spacetime and that causality is absolute—may not correspond to reality.

## Comments

- When I was an undergraduate, I once thought that neuroscientists were somewhat like astronomers. They seemed similar in that both explore unknown spaces and discover new facts, and in that both are willing to make bold speculations when necessary in areas where sufficient evidence does not yet exist. Reading this book also felt a little like drifting through space. It moves dizzyingly back and forth between established facts and bold hypotheses, but in the end, that was exactly what made it more fun.
- The idea that the purpose of the brain is not to perceive the world exactly as it is, but to interpret it in ways advantageous for survival, seems at least somewhat right. In other words, the world we experience is not fixed. If so, there may also be no single correct way to build a world model, nor a single correct final form for a world model. Ultimately, thinking about world models leads back to the unanswerable question of "What are we modeling the world for?"—in other words, how should we define the objective function?
  - Thinking a little further about the objective function, animals have sensory systems that differ substantially from ours. In some cases, such as infrared, the sensory modality resembles human vision but operates over a different range; in others, such as sensing the Earth's magnetic field, animals possess senses that humans do not have at all. Then is the human way of perceiving the world really optimal for maximizing intelligence? Have humans already been sufficiently optimized through evolution to maximize intelligence, discarding senses such as magnetoreception because they contribute little to greater intelligence? If so, should we simply continue making machines sense the world in human-like ways in order to increase their intelligence? Or is there some combination of senses that would be better than ours? For example, perhaps adding just one particular sense to the traditional five could produce a world model far superior to a human one.
  - Conversely, perhaps subtracting something rather than adding something could be better. For example, is the tendency to interpret every phenomenon causally really optimal even for the objective of maximizing intelligence? A tendency to interpret the world causally may have been advantageous for survival, but could it instead become a constraint on achieving a higher level of intelligence? Much like an autoregressive transformer that generates the next token but struggles to escape a false premise introduced at an earlier step.
- It is difficult to believe all the claims in this book. In particular, the more central the claim, the blurrier the boundary becomes between established theory and the author's speculation. Paradoxically, however, this was exactly why the book gave me so much to think about. Rather than teaching me a coherent theory, I think it gave me an opportunity to question assumptions I had taken for granted in different ways.

---

## Takeaways

- Since the world is not fixed, there is no single correct answer for a world model either. Therefore, a good world model cannot be evaluated solely by how perfectly it replicates reality. The information it needs and the way it should represent that information may vary depending on what the world model is intended for.
- Increasing intelligence may not necessarily require feeding in more information. What matters more may be which information is sensed, which information is discarded, and which relationships are treated as fundamental assumptions.
