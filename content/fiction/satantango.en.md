## Metadata

- Title: Satantango
- Author: László Krasznahorkai (won the Nobel Prize in Literature 👍)
- Publication year: 1985

---

Against the rather ordinary and pessimistic backdrop of a decaying rural village in Eastern Europe, we meet people doing ridiculous things. The author describes this black comedy in apocalyptic terms. Everything is rotting, death seeps into every narrow crack, and everyone is hanging by a thread. As I read, the village began to feel as if it existed somewhere, in both time and space, not long before the end of the world.

But this village is not hell. It is not some surreal, hell-like place, but a realistic one. There used to be a factory here, and there used to be jobs. And because of that, the people never stop hoping that life will get better. Even when that hope is crushed, they are only disappointed for a moment before, without tiring, finding something new to hope for. And that hope, without fail, is crushed again.

Hope gives birth to despair, and despair gives birth to hope again. This cycle becomes the ultimate despair, keeping everything from even reaching an end by preventing it from simply falling into the abyss of ruin once and for all. In the end, this village becomes a place where “even the saints stopped while trying to step out of the paintings.” The saints are there. They just cannot get out of the paintings. (Once again, this is not a place where surreal events happen.) Like dancers in a tango taking six steps forward and then six steps back, the people move back and forth between hope and despair, wandering across the flat plane of this world.

The only character who escapes this world is Estike. Estike is a young girl who suffers psychological abuse from her family and eventually kills herself. She takes her own life believing that she will go to heaven, guided there by angels. And then the girl’s corpse rises high into the air and flies up into the sky. Once again, this is not a place where surreal events happen. So is Estike’s ascension a miracle? Or at least a message of hope? One thing worth noting is that Estike is not resurrected. So, to borrow the words of one of the characters, it is something that could, with a low probability, actually happen. Nor is Estike’s ascension described beautifully. It is portrayed almost as if the wind had snatched up a scrap of cloth and carried it away. Whether to read this event as a sign of hope is up to the reader.

_Satantango_ is full of wonderful sentences. Beautiful but unpleasant, subtle but primitive, brilliant but gloomy—I found myself thinking that perhaps these sentences themselves touch on the novel’s theme. How are we supposed to read sentences that constantly clash with one another, weaving together a vast, multilayered despair? Is Satan’s tango mesmerizing, or ominous?
