## Bibliographic Information

- Title: Selected Novellas and Short Stories by Mo Yan (Minumsa, Korean edition)
- Author: Mo Yan (莫言) (won the Nobel Prize 🏅)
- Publication year: 2016 for the Minumsa edition; the stories were written between 1985 and 2000.

### Mo Yan Is a Nobel Laureate

Mo Yan won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2012. The Swedish Academy gave the following reason for the award.

> The 2012 Nobel Prize in Literature is awarded to the Chinese writer Mo Yan, who merges folk tales, history, and contemporary reality through hallucinatory realism.

Two ideas in this citation capture Mo Yan's writing: hallucinatory realism, and the merging of folk tales, history, and contemporary reality. By unpacking these two ideas, I want to leave a short note on what I have come to understand about him.

### Mo Yan's Writing Is Fantastical

Mo Yan's stories often venture unmistakably into fantasy. People pass through a well into another world, have a wonderful feast with people who have only one nostril, and then return. A child becomes a spirit-like being who lives on iron. At times, it almost feels like reading Western fantasy.

Yet these stories are also strikingly realistic, because they all begin in ordinary life. The people who enter another world were having dinner with their families. The child who becomes a spirit was living in a government-run nursery in China.

The tragedy makes this realism even harder to ignore. Why did those people go to another world? Because they had been going hungry for a long time. Why did the child become an iron-eating spirit? His parents were sent away to work on a collective project and never came back. Thrown out of the nursery, he wandered around with nothing to eat until he finally learned to eat iron. (He starts by eating discarded rails from China's railway construction projects, then moves on to attacking lines still under construction, becoming a source of terror for the workers.) Realities like these run through Mo Yan's stories, making them difficult to read as just ordinary fantasy.

The tension between reality and hallucination builds, then vanishes like a bubble as the stories lead us back to ordinary life near the end. But the tension is never fully resolved. A hallucination can only be recognized as such once it is over, so returning to reality actually completes it. Its ending makes clear that the inexplicable experience was a hallucination, rather than a new reality. The characters then have to carry that experience with them as they make their way through the life that remains. Just like the readers, who have to return to their own lives after finishing the story!

### Mo Yan's Writing Is Distinctly Chinese

Mo Yan shows reality as it is: grueling labor, the gap between rich and poor, bodies maimed in accidents. As I mentioned earlier, he pays particular attention to Chinese realities. His stories deal with issues that run through modern Chinese history, including birth restrictions, collective labor, large state-led development projects, and rural poverty. Rather than treating these as political arguments, he works them into ordinary people's lives. China's social structures appear as concrete events that take someone's parents away, threaten someone's livelihood, or leave someone injured.

His hallucinations are rooted in Chinese folk tales too. This inheritance gives his fantasy a distinct cultural identity. Supernatural beings such as spirits and ghosts appear, and the boundaries between humans and animals, reality and fantasy, break down. These stories bring traditional Chinese tales and ghost stories to mind. The characters are amazed by what happens, but sometimes they also take it in stride—as if to show just how familiar Chinese folk tales are to them.

So calling Mo Yan's writing Chinese means more than saying that it is set in China and features Chinese characters. Both the reality his stories begin in and the fantasy through which they leave it are rooted in Chinese history, culture, and contemporary life.

### + Mo Yan's Writing Has Range

All this might make Mo Yan sound primarily like a writer of social criticism, but his work is much more varied.*(Author's note. Mo Yan avoids topics that would upset the Chinese government. He is also known for his consistently ambivalent stance on state censorship. Western intellectuals criticize him for this. Still, I sometimes wonder whether this is about as much as he can do from where he stands.)*

For a start, his themes vary widely. He explores romantic questions such as what value fantasy has in a monotonous modern society, or whether love can save an individual. He also writes about desire, obsession, death, and fate—themes that cut across time and place.

The moods are just as varied. His stories are solemn, warm, mysterious, brutal, thrilling, horrifying, heartbreaking, comic, melancholy, lyrical, tender, exuberant, peaceful, and sad. Several of these feelings can mingle freely within a single story. He builds anticipation, then makes us laugh at something absurd. We follow a ridiculous character, only to suddenly feel for their predicament. Mo Yan seems to want to show life itself, without keeping comedy and tragedy in separate boxes.

### Conclusion

If I had to sum up this collection, I would put it like this: distinctly Chinese stories that move freely, in strange and unsettling ways, between fairy-tale fantasy and reality that jolts you wide awake.
